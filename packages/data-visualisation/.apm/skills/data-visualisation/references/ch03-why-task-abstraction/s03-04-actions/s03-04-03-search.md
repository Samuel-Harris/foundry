# 3.4.3 Search

## Four search types

Every high-level analyze case needs the user to search for elements of interest as a mid-level goal. Search splits four ways by whether the identity and the location of the target are already known [p.78]. A margin note adds that "find" is often used as a synonym in descriptions of search tasks, implying a successful outcome [p.78].

<!-- verbatim p.68 -->
> At the middle level, search can be classified according to whether the identity and location of targets are known or not: both are known with lookup, the target is known but its location is not for locate, the location is known but the target is not for browse, and neither the target nor the location are known for explore.
<!-- /verbatim -->

| | Location known | Location unknown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Target known** | lookup [p.78] | locate [p.78] |
| **Target unknown** | browse [pp.78-79] | explore [p.79] |

<a id="s03.04.03.01"></a>

### Lookup

<!-- verbatim p.78 -->
> If users already know both what they’re looking for and where it is, then the search type is simply lookup.
<!-- /verbatim -->

Example: in a tree vis of mammal ancestry, a user looking up humans gets to the right spot quickly by remembering the classification: live young rather than eggs (platypus) or a pouch (kangaroos), then primates [p.78].

<a id="s03.04.03.02"></a>

### Locate

<!-- verbatim p.78 -->
> To find a known target at an unknown location, the search type is locate: that is, find out where the specific object is.
<!-- /verbatim -->

Example: the same user, not knowing where rabbits are, must look in several places before locating them as lagomorphs, not rodents [p.78].

<a id="s03.04.03.03"></a>

### Browse

When the exact identity of the target is not known in advance, it may be specified by characteristics [p.78]:

<!-- verbatim p.78 -->
> In this case, users are searching for one or more items that fit some kind of specification, such as matching up with a particular range of attribute values.
<!-- /verbatim -->

When users do not know exactly what they are looking for but do have a location in mind, the search type is browse [pp.78-79]. Examples: searching within a particular subtree of a tree vis for leaf nodes with few siblings; in a line graph of several companies' share prices over the past month, examining each line's price on June 15 [p.79].

<a id="s03.04.03.04"></a>

### Explore

When users are not even sure of the location, the search type is explore [p.79].

<!-- verbatim p.79 -->
> It entails searching for characteristics without regard to their location, often beginning from an overview of everything.
<!-- /verbatim -->

Examples: outliers in a scatterplot, anomalous spikes or periodic patterns in a time-series line graph, unanticipated spatially dependent patterns in a choropleth map [p.79].
