# 3.7 Analyzing and Deriving: Examples

Three short worked examples of applying the what–why–how framework: a comparative analysis of two vis tools, deriving a single attribute (an importance measure for trees, to decide which branches to show when summarising topology), and deriving many new attributes from a spatial fluid dynamics dataset so that features of interest are easy to find [p.84].

## Children

- [3.7.1 Comparing Two Idioms](s03-07-01-comparing-two-idioms.md) · ~376 words. **Use when** comparing or critiquing two idioms that share the same what and why but differ in how; worked on SpaceTree and TreeJuxtaposer for locating and identifying a path in a large tree to present it [pp.84-85]
- [3.7.2 Deriving One Attribute](s03-07-02-deriving-one-attribute.md) · ~450 words. **Use when** a large tree or network must be summarised by filtering on a derived, globally computed importance attribute such as the Strahler number [pp.85-87]
- [3.7.3 Deriving Many New Attributes](s03-07-03-deriving-many-new-attributes.md) · ~538 words. **Use when** features in a spatial field are hard to see in physical space and could be found in juxtaposed plots of pairs of derived quantitative attributes, linked by colour highlighting [pp.87-89]

## Disambiguation

- **Deriving One Attribute vs Deriving Many New Attributes.** 3.7.2 derives one quantitative attribute on a tree's nodes and uses it to filter (reduce) for a summarize task; 3.7.3 derives many quantitative attributes from a spatial field and encodes pairs of them in juxtaposed, linked views to find features [p.87] [p.89].
- **Comparing Two Idioms vs Deriving One Attribute.** Both take trees as input; 3.7.1 compares two whole idioms for a path-finding task, while 3.7.2 is a chained sequence in which a derived attribute drives filtering [p.85] [p.87].
