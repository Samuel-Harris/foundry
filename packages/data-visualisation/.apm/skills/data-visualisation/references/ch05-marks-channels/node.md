# 5 Marks and Channels

The building blocks of every visual encoding: marks are geometric elements (points, lines, areas) that depict items or links, and channels (position, colour, size, shape and so on) control their appearance [pp.120-121]. Channels split into magnitude channels for ordered attributes and identity channels for categorical attributes, each ranked by effectiveness [p.120] [pp.126-127]. The rankings are primarily based on accuracy but also take discriminability, separability, popout and grouping into account [p.128] [p.131]. A final section adds that perception rests on relative rather than absolute judgements [p.137]. Start here to pick a channel for an attribute, to justify a channel choice, or to critique an encoding that misuses one.

## Disambiguation

- **The Big Picture vs Using Marks and Channels.** 5.1 states the rule in one paragraph and points at the figure; 5.4 gives both ranked lists in full with the book's wording, and is the one to load when actually choosing channels [p.120] [pp.126-127].
- **Defining Marks and Channels vs Using Marks and Channels.** 5.3 says what marks and channels are and which channels are identity or magnitude; 5.4 says which channel to use for which attribute and in what order of preference [pp.120-127].
- **Using Marks and Channels vs Channel Effectiveness.** 5.4 gives the rankings; 5.5 justifies them and adds the limits a ranking alone hides, such as too few distinguishable levels or channels that interfere when combined [pp.126-131].
- **Channel Effectiveness vs Relative versus Absolute Judgements.** 5.5 compares channels with each other; 5.6 explains why the same channel is judged more precisely when marks are aligned or framed, and why colour depends on its surroundings [p.128] [pp.137-139].
