# 6.6 Resolution over Immersion

## The rule

<!-- verbatim p.159 -->
> Pixels are precious: if you are faced with a trade-off between resolution and immersion, resolution usually is far more important.
<!-- /verbatim -->

## What immersion is

Immersive environments simulate realistic interaction and perception as closely as possible, with stereo imagery delivered separately to each eye and full six-degree-of-freedom head and position tracking; the usual hardware is head-mounted displays or small rooms with rear-projected walls, floor and ceiling [p.159]. Immersion is most useful when a sense of presence matters to the task [p.159].

## Price one: resolution

With current hardware, resolution (available pixels divided by display area) trades against immersion (the feeling of presence); immersive displays cannot show as many pixels as state-of-the-art desktop displays of equivalent area [p.159].

<!-- verbatim p.159 -->
> The number of pixels available on a computer display is a limited resource that is usually the most critical constraint in vis design. Thus, it is extremely rare that immersion is worth the cost in resolution.
<!-- /verbatim -->

## Price two: workflow integration

- **Location.** Immersive displays are almost always special-purpose settings away from the user's own workspace, down the hall or in another building [pp.159-160].
- **Standing.** Users usually stand, which is physically taxing over long periods compared with sitting at a desk [p.160].
- **No usual tools.** The most critical problem: without mouse and keyboard, standard applications such as web browsing, email, text and spreadsheet editing and other analysis packages are unusable in most cases and awkward at best. A desktop vis system integrates with the usual workflow and allows fast switching between vis and other applications [p.160].

## When immersion might be justified

Phobia desensitisation through virtual reality needs presence, but it is not vis, because it simulates reality rather than visually encoding information [p.160]. The most likely helpful case for vis is when the abstraction includes 3D spatial data, and even then the designer should weigh presence against lower resolution and lost workflow integration [p.160].

<!-- verbatim p.160 -->
> It is very rare that immersion would be necessary for nonspatial, abstract data. Using 3D for visual encoding of abstract data is the uncommon case that needs careful justification. The use of an immersive display in this case would require even more careful justification.
<!-- /verbatim -->
