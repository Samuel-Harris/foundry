# 10.2.1 Color Vision

## Receptors

The retina has two kinds of receptors. Rods contribute to vision only in low light and give low-resolution black-and-white information, so the book sets them aside. Cones are the main sensors in normal lighting; there are three types, each with peak sensitivity at a different wavelength of visible light [pp.244-245].

## Opponent colour channels

<!-- verbatim p.245 -->
> The visual system immediately processes these signals into three opponent color channels: one from red to green, one from blue to yellow, and one from black and white encoding luminance information.
<!-- /verbatim -->

The luminance channel carries high-resolution edge information; the red–green and blue–yellow channels are lower resolution [p.245].

<!-- verbatim p.245 -->
> This split between luminance and chromaticity—what most people informally call would normally call color—is a central issue in visual encoding design.
<!-- /verbatim -->

The doubled wording ("informally call would normally call") is in the book [p.245].

## Colour deficiency

Opponent channel theory explains colour blindness, which in its most common form affects around 8% of men. The book prefers the term colour deficiency: the red–green channel is reduced or absent, while the blue–yellow channel works fully [p.245].

- The two common forms of red–green deficiency, deuteranopia and protanopia, are both sex linked [p.245].
- Tritanopia impairs the blue–yellow channel; it is extremely rare and not sex linked [p.245].
