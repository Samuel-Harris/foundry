# 11.2 Why Change?

## Five options for handling complexity

Datasets are often large and complex enough that showing everything at once in one static view would produce overwhelming visual clutter [p.269]. There are five major options for handling that complexity, and a view that changes over time is one of them; the five are not mutually exclusive and can be combined [p.269].

The other four, per the margin note [p.269]:

- deriving new data, discussed in Chapter 3 [p.269];
- faceting the data by partitioning it into multiple juxtaposed views or superimposed layers, in Chapter 12 [p.269];
- reducing the amount of data shown within a view, in Chapter 13 [p.269];
- embedding focus and context information together within a single view, in Chapter 14 [p.269].

## Why change over time comes first

<!-- verbatim p.269 -->
> Changing the view over time is the most obvious, most popular, and most flexible choice in vis design.
<!-- /verbatim -->

<!-- verbatim p.269 -->
> The most fundamental breakthrough of vis on a computer display compared with printed on paper is the possibility of interactivity: a view that changes over time can dynamically respond to user input, rather than being limited to a static visual encoding.
<!-- /verbatim -->

All interactive idioms involve a view that changes over time [p.269].
